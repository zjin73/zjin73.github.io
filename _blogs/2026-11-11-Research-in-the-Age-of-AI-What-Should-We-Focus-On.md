## Recognizing the Value of the Human Element

> We should pay greater attention to individuality and expression. These are gifts that everyone is born with, but not everyone takes the time to develop them.
>
> Of course, pursuing novelty for its own sake is not a good choice in research. What I mean is that we should develop our individuality in the way we think about problems. Once you hand the process of thinking itself over to AI, I can foresee your research becoming less and less like research. I once saw someone say on social media that what AI does best is recombination. So if you want to know whether there is anything innovative at the intersection of A and B and you hand that question to AI, it will almost certainly produce a long list of possibilities. Recently, a friend used AI to write a research proposal for his PhD applications. Afterward, he said plainly, “I cannot achieve the goals it set for me.”
>
> What am I trying to say? AI must be used as a tool that assists people, not as a humanlike substitute for them.
> When we ask AI questions about our research, its answers depend heavily on how we frame those questions. The very act of asking can give it room to make things up. Ask AI to find reasons why something makes sense, and it can give you ten thousand; ask why it does not make sense, and another ten thousand are waiting. This is how AI can mislead us.

## Placing Greater Emphasis on Systematic Learning

> AI may now appear to know everything under the sun—to understand both the heavens above and the earth below, and even to know five hundred years of history and foresee the next five hundred. But all of that knowledge comes from the data it has been fed. Anyone who has trained a model knows one thing: “Data is all you need.” In other words, if you expect AI to give you correct knowledge, you must ensure that no dirty data enters during training or inference and that no dirty data comes out. Only then can the resulting knowledge be considered reliable. Reality, however, is very different. As noted above, AI has always been good at stitching things together, and stitching fragments of knowledge together can easily produce false interpretations. Some supervisors even use ChatGPT, DeepSeek, or Doubao to advise their students. Imagine a professor who has spent years working on stochastic control relying on AI prompts to offer unconstrained guidance on embodied-intelligence research. The absurdity is obvious. The problems surrounding academic supervision are, of course, far more complicated and deserve many blog posts of their own; I will return to them in future updates.
>
> In our time, the ability to judge whether something is right or wrong has become especially important. The standards behind that judgment—and even the intuition behind it—cannot be replaced by AI. They can only be built gradually as we construct our own systems of knowledge.
>
> This is why a coherent, non-fragmented body of knowledge is so valuable in the age of AI. AI may help accelerate the process of building one. For example, after watching an open course from Stanford or MIT, I can ask AI about a concept that still puzzles me. Compared with searching through blogs and forums or starting a discussion there, this can be much more efficient.

## Learning to Communicate

*Under the impact of AI, questions are everywhere and so are polished explanations—but much of this content is merely assembled token by token. It carries no feeling; it is only a jigsaw puzzle made of terms. To become a capable researcher, we must learn to communicate more effectively. Most importantly, we must learn how to define a problem correctly and with good taste. This matters enormously, and I am still exploring how to do it. Without a good question, research has no anchor. No matter how many results we produce, what problem have we actually solved? How do all the small pieces connect? These are also questions of expression, and expression itself rests on our understanding and on the structure of our knowledge. The ability to define a problem may therefore become the touchstone for researchers in the age of AI.*
